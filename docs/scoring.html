Evaluation

Evaluating a prediction function

The primary score measures error in joint predictions of sensor readings. Lower is better.

A predictor returns an empirical distribution of possible trajectories. The evaluator compares it with independent physical realizations on programs fixed before grading.

The score

A case is one action program and its measurement queries, run from the laboratory's prepared state. Within a case, a group selects scalar readings to score together: for example, one field at all 13 sensors on an instrument at several times. Each selected reading is one coordinate of the group's vector.

For a fixed case c and group g, let fik and ojk be forecast and observed readings at coordinate k. “Scaled” means dividing both by the same positive, predeclared scale sk, expressed in that reading's native units:

xik=fiksk , yjk=ojksk

The resulting vectors are dimensionless. Scales determine how much a difference in each physical quantity contributes; they are fixed before grading, not estimated from the submitted predictions or grading truths. For a group with d coordinates, use the root mean square distance:

D(a,b)= 1d∑k=1d(ak−bk)2

With M forecast members and N independent truth realizations, the energy score for this group in this case is:

Ecg= 1MN ∑i=1M ∑j=1N D(xi,yj)− 12M2 ∑i=1M ∑ℓ=1M D(xi,xℓ)

The second term accounts for the predictor's spread. The implementation includes all ordered forecast pairs, including self-pairs. It scores the returned empirical distribution; finite sampling can therefore affect the score even for the correct stochastic process.

If case c has Gc groups, its score Sc is their equal mean. The primary score S is the equal mean over the suite's C cases:

Sc=1Gc ∑g=1GcEcg , S=1C∑c=1CSc

Groups have equal weight even when they contain different numbers of coordinates. Cases have equal weight even when they contain different numbers of groups.

The current suites request 64 forecast members and use two saved independent truth realizations per program. The score is dimensionless and has no universal “percent accurate” interpretation.

CRPS and framework reward

For a single scalar reading, the same energy-score formula with d = 1 and no rescaling becomes the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), averaged over the truth realizations: the distance is simply the absolute difference. Joint energy extends this calculation to vectors of readings.

Physim reports marginal CRPS separately for individual readings, averaging over requested times for each port and sensor slot. These diagnostics retain native units: field readings and global means have field-value units, while global variances have squared units. They help locate prediction errors; averaging marginal CRPS is not the joint score.

The Verifiers reward is computed from the primary joint energy score S defined above:

R=11+S

Lower joint energy gives higher reward. CRPS does not enter this reward mapping, and the reward is not a percentage accuracy.

Building and validating a suite

A suite fixes a preparation, action/query programs, score groups and scales, independent grading truth, and an aggregation rule. BF provides a worked example: its slowly relaxing trail field can affect later activator motion through bilinear feedback. An evaluation should therefore measure responses over time and space, including the trail as well as the activator.

Choose a repeatable starting state

The BF recipe begins with the saved endpoint of a 2,500-time-unit simulation. Both instrument arrays are centered using the position of the maximum activator value, and each source is at its array's center. Every experimental program restores those fields and poses, then draws fresh ongoing noise. This holds the initial physical situation fixed while allowing repeated outcomes to vary.

Turn interventions into cases

The recipe compares no intervention, weak and strong activator pulses, three trail-pulse amplitudes, an activator pulse followed by a trail pulse, a delayed activator pulse, a fast-inhibitor pulse, a translated probe, and a dilated probe: eleven programs in total. Each is examined with three independent noise realizations, using both full fields and sensor readings to choose useful observations.

For example, act_then_trail applies a strong activator pulse for five time units, then a trail pulse at time 10. In BF's anonymous port mapping these are ports 1 and 2:

actions = [
    {"t": 0, "kind": "inject", "device": 0, "port": 1, "amp": 0.3, "dur": 5},
    {"t": 10, "kind": "inject", "device": 0, "port": 2, "amp": 0.01, "dur": 5},
]
times = [0, 2, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 22, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50]
queries = [{"sensor": s, "t": times} for s in ("device0", "device1", "global")]

Those actions and queries define one case. It returns 15 observation times from each sensor; the score groups select particular readings from those arrays. Comparing its response with the activator-only and no-intervention programs helps establish what the second pulse changes.

Choose measurements and scales

BF uses four groups in every case. The physical field names below are available to the suite author; the agent sees numbered ports. All sensor slots of the named instrument are included at each selected time.

For the slow-memory group, one forecast member becomes a 65-coordinate vector, with every entry divided by 0.05. A trail discrepancy of 0.01 therefore becomes 0.2 in scaled units, as does an activator discrepancy of 0.2 with scale 1.0. These choices make the small-amplitude trail contribute meaningfully alongside the activator. The scales are fixed from development evidence before generating grading truth.

For this case, calculate four energy scores and average them to obtain Sc. Repeat for all eleven cases and average their scores to obtain S, then compute R = 1 / (1 + S). The global readings and unselected local readings remain available for marginal CRPS diagnostics.

Check what the suite can detect

The mechanism check compares a trail pulse with no pulse, then repeats that comparison with bilinear feedback removed. It uses the same starting fields and paired noise within each comparison to isolate the feedback's contribution. Removing a reaction term is a privileged scientific control, not an action available to the agent.

After the programs, groups, and scales are frozen, the builder generates two new grading truths per case. Their noise seeds are separate from development runs and from the following diagnostic forecasts:

Diagnostic predictorWhat the comparison tests
Full native simulator, independent noiseThe score obtained by the correct stochastic process with finite samples.
Native simulator ignoring actionsWhether the selected measurements distinguish interventions from unperturbed evolution.
Native simulator with bilinear feedback removedWhether the score is sensitive to this physical mechanism.
Native simulator with incorrect instrument positionsWhether spatial placement matters to the selected readings.
Initial readings repeated at every timeWhether a predictor must model evolution rather than preserve the initial observation.

These comparisons test the suite's sensitivity. If removing a mechanism leaves scores unchanged across repeated comparisons, the chosen cases or measurements do not establish sensitivity to that mechanism. Installation checks then verify the prediction interface, reproduce saved reference scores, and exercise the simulator and environment. The preparation workflow links the runnable BF recipe, mechanism check, bundle builder, and validation commands.

Eval-ready adds an exact starting state, apparatus, suite, truth data, and validation evidence that the environment can run. It describes this particular preparation and suite, rather than every use of the genome.

What the current score can establish

Controlled predictors show sensitivity to omitted pulses, ignored probe movements, and lost spatial structure. The original reference's primary groups also have a known limitation: two programs select coarse timestamps that miss some fine switch and recovery timing. Full-array marginal diagnostics expose those errors.

Two truths per program and one preparation per world provide limited evidence about uncertainty calibration or generalization. A low aggregate score establishes performance on the selected measurements; it does not by itself show that an agent recovered all of the underlying physics.

The published worlds, preparations, programs, and truths are openly available reference material. Results on them are development results. Claims about performance on unfamiliar systems require appropriately held-out worlds or preparations, with private challenge data kept in a separate grader store.
