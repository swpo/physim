## Nearest published work for recent results

- **PPS cells, spores, budding and germination (`20261026T0215-c29-pps-lineage`): known.** Schmickl, Stefanec &
  Crailsheim 2016 (Sci Rep 6:37969, PMC5346932, opened) describe the same life cycle. Spores of 14-22 particles grow
  into ring cells of 23-60 (mean ~41.5, typical ~48). A cell divides at ~61 particles into two cells, or throws out
  one or two spores. Population growth is logistic. Our 68-75-particle median parent at a persistent split and the
  bud/germinate strips match this. What the ledger adds is a merge-aware particle-identity genealogy (generation 2-3)
  and the beta=0 control. The paper does not count generations.
- **Handedness.** The paper states that a positive change of phi is a turn to the right (first-person view), i.e.
  clockwise. This is why the counter-clockwise reading in our sighting gave only coarsening blobs. Anyone porting PPS
  must use the clockwise sign. (The tool summary rendered the rule as alpha + beta (L - R); the paper's own equation
  is alpha + beta N sign(R - L) per LITERATURE.md. I could not check the exact typography: UNVERIFIED.)
- **Density thresholds (new for us).** The paper reports that cells replicate only above about 0.054 particles per
  unit area (infertile below about 0.032). Between 0.046 and 0.054, cells often die in failed replication attempts,
  and the population scales linearly with density above it. All our runs used 0.08.
- **Swimming condensates that dissolve (`20261026T0227-c29-kernel-ring-screen`, context).** Jambon-Puillet et al. 2024
  (Nat Commun 15:3919, opened): phase-separated droplets swim up gradients toward conditions that dissolve them
  ("dialytaxis", Marangoni-driven). Our ringed cells swim toward fresh substrate and need it to live, which is the
  opposite sign: motion toward the resource that maintains the body. The nearest model remains Demarchi et al. 2023
  (PRL 130:128401; cited in that paper's reference list and opened by c27 in the previous check). That relation is
  *variant*: Demarchi's condensates swim and position themselves but have no wall.
